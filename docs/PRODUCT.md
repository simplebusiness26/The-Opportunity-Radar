# What Radar is for

> Given what is changing in the world, what we already possess, what we have
> previously learned, our present resources and our strategic goals — where
> should we deploy our next unit of effort for the highest expected return?

That is the whole product. Everything else is machinery for answering it
honestly.

## What it is not

- **Not an idea generator.** Ideas are cheap and Radar produces none. It reads
  evidence and tells you what the evidence supports.
- **Not a trend feed.** A trend is not an opportunity, and momentum without
  spending is not demand.
- **Not a validation score.** A single number that says "83% validated" is a
  confidence trick. Radar shows attractiveness and confidence separately,
  because they answer different questions.
- **Not a research chatbot.** Ask Radar answers from your own records or refuses.

## The five things it combines

Any one of these alone is a commodity. Together they are difficult to copy,
because two of them are *yours*.

1. **External intelligence** — what is changing outside, deduplicated to the
   point where the counts mean something.
2. **Internal intelligence** — what this particular team already has. This is
   the half nobody else knows, and it is why Radar can say "you could ship this
   in a fortnight and they could not".
3. **Execution memory** — what happened last time you tried something like this.
4. **Resource allocation** — what deserves the next unit of effort, given days
   and money that actually exist.
5. **Closed-loop learning** — whether the recommendation was right, fed back in.

## The answers it is allowed to give

Sometimes the right answer is "build this". Often it is not:

- **Improve what you already have.** An existing product with users is usually
  a better bet than a new one with none.
- **Productise that component.** You already built it; someone else needs it.
- **Wait.** The evidence points somewhere, but not yet.
- **Do nothing.** `NO HIGH-CONFIDENCE OPPORTUNITY CURRENTLY WARRANTS ACTION` is
  a real output, rendered exactly like that, with the cheapest next actions
  ranked by what they would resolve.

A system that always has an exciting answer is not measuring anything.

## What honesty means here, concretely

- Missing data is `insufficient_evidence`, never zero. "No evidence they will
  pay" and "evidence they will not pay" are opposite findings.
- AI-derived material never counts toward independence or confidence.
- Confidence is capped below certainty. No forecast about the future is certain.
- Calibration refuses below eight completed projects and says how many more it
  needs.
- Estimates are ranges. A single number implies a precision the evidence does
  not support.
- Every claim in an investigation cites the evidence it rests on, or is dropped.
- Seeded sample data is labelled DEMO wherever it appears.
- Radar recommends; a person decides. Rejecting, committing and spending are
  owner decisions, and the system records what it recommended at the time so it
  can be judged later.

## Who it is for

A small team, or one person, deciding what to do next with limited time. Not an
enterprise portfolio committee: the allocation model assumes the scarcest
resource is attention, and that whoever reads the answer is also the person who
would do the work.
